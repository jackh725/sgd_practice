## 音频原文 (Transcript)

```text
Narration: Three teachers are discussing free online courses.

Speaker1: I’ve been looking into the growth of free online courses lately. The fact that there are now so many available is really promising. Don’t get me wrong—online teaching requires a lot of effort to design and deliver well—but overall, I think it’s a very good idea, especially when you consider accessibility. Many people simply cannot attend traditional school because of their financial situation, so these courses can be a lifeline. Nowadays, it’s impressive how many well-known universities offer free online courses… but I’ve noticed a real gap between offering and uptake. I can hardly believe so few people who enroll see it through to the end.

Speaker2: I think you’ve hit on a key point about completion. But even before that, there’s an issue with initial trust. From talking to some learners, a common reaction is that some people think it is too good to be true, and they doubt whether it is a scam. That initial skepticism can prevent them from even clicking ‘enroll’. Some others simply think it is completely useless.

Speaker3: Trust is one thing, but in my experience, the bigger hurdle is practical. Many students—or rather, potential learners—simply do not have the time to commit to these online courses. They’re often juggling jobs and family. Universities could definitely do more to attract them by offering flexibility. For example, they could reschedule these courses to other times, or better yet, make them entirely self-paced to attract more outsiders to join. If you really want to learn, those who have a clear personal or professional goal—the real desire to study—are often the most likely to complete the courses.

Speaker1: Exactly, and without that external structure, even motivated learners can hit a wall. Some people start with enthusiasm but quickly get discouraged by the tedious coursework. They discover that a large amount of independent work is required, and it’s easy to fall behind when you’re alone. And because these courses are free, you don’t face any financial punishment for dropping out.

Speaker2: That’s a crucial insight about commitment. The absence of financial consequence really does remove a key layer of accountability. In a traditional course, the sunk cost makes you think twice before giving up. Here, the only penalty is to yourself—your own goals and time invested. Perhaps we need to find other ways to build in accountability, like peer study groups or non-financial rewards for milestones.

Speaker3: Lack of promotion is also an issue. Right now, the school has just posted the online courses on its website, and the promotion effort is obviously not strong enough. Additionally, some students prefer practical courses to theoretical ones, and we choose a course not because it is easy to pass, but because we are interested in it.
```

表示在线学习需要付出大量努力。

课程供给与实际完成率之间的差距。

-Solutions（解决方案）

1.Peer study groups or non-financial rewards for milestones.

建立同伴学习小组，或为完成阶段目标设置非经济类奖励。

2.We need to find ways to build in accountability.

我们需要找到建立责任感的方法。

Speaker Three（male）

-Learner Dilemma（学习者困境）

同意另外两人关于免费在线课程存在问题的观点。

很多潜在学习者没有足够时间投入这些在线课程。

有明确目标的学习者最有可能完成课程。

4.Lack of promotion for the online courses.

在线课程宣传不足这一点。

-Optimization Direction（优化方向）

1.Universities offering flexibility to attract more learners.

相信大学提供灵活性可以吸引更多学习者。

2.Solution of building community and alternative incentives.

建立学习社区和替代激励措施的解决方案。

3.Developers need to design with the learner’s real life in mind.

总结说课程设计者需要考虑学习者的真实生活情况来设计课程。
